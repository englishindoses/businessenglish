Lesson 9

Project Updates and Deadlines

Reporting progress, explaining delays, and talking about deadlines clearly and professionally.

🎯 Objectives

What You'll Learn Today

  • Report progress clearly: what's done, what's in progress, and what's next.
  • Explain delays and ask for what you need, using key project vocabulary.
  • Use the present perfect simple and continuous, and by and until for deadlines.

📖 Reading

A Weekly Update on the New HR System

Camila is leading the project to move her company to a new HR system. Read her weekly update meeting with Rafael, the Head of HR, and then do the activities below.

Rafael:Morning, Camila. Can you give me a quick update on the new HR system?

Camila:Sure. The good news first. We've reached the first milestone: all the employee records have been moved to the new system. We've completed the testing phase, and the first deliverable, the user guide for line managers, has been sent to all the department heads.

Rafael:Great. And what's happening at the moment?

Camila:We've been making good progress on the training programme. The team has been working on the training videos since Monday, and we've recorded four of the ten so far. Overall, we're on schedule for the launch on 1 May.

Rafael:That's good to hear. What's next?

Camila:Our main priority this week is the training timetable for managers. We estimate that it will take about two weeks to organise all the sessions, and we expect to finish by the end of the month, so we should meet the deadline.

Rafael:Any problems?

Camila:Two, I'm afraid. We're behind schedule on the payroll module because of the supplier. They've been having technical problems for about a week, and their support team is away until Wednesday. So the payroll integration has been postponed until next month. There's also been a delay with the data checks, due to staff shortages in IT.

Rafael:OK. What do you need from me?

Camila:Two things. First, would it be possible to extend the deadline for the data checks by one week? And second, could we allocate more resources to the data checks? If we can't, we may need to reduce the scope of the project and leave the holiday-booking feature until the summer.

Rafael:I'd rather avoid that. I'll speak to IT today. Anything else?

Camila:Just one more thing. The line managers have been asking for extra training. Can we agree to prioritise their requests over the other departments' for now?

Rafael:Yes, that makes sense. Send me the updated schedule by Thursday, and I'll have an answer on the resources for you by Friday.

💡 Notice

Camila's update follows a clear order: what's done, what's in progress, what's next, any problems, and what she needs. She uses "we've completed" for finished work and "we've been working" for work that is still going on. Notice also the difference between "send me the schedule by Thursday" and "their support team is away until Wednesday".

✅ What's Done

Work that is finished.

🔄 In Progress

Work that is still going on, and how it's going.

➡️ What's Next

Plans, estimates and expected finish dates.

⚠️ Delays and Problems

Explaining what's late and why.

🙋 Asking for What You Need

Asking for more time, more people or changes to the plan.

💡 Words that go together

Learn project words with the words they usually go with. These combinations are what make an update sound natural.

  • meet / miss / extend a deadline.
  • reach a milestone.
  • make progress (not "do progress").
  • on / behind / ahead of schedule.
  • allocate resources to something.
  • reduce the scope of a project.

Grammar Review

Present Perfect Simple vs Continuous

Both tenses connect the past to now, which is exactly what a project update does. The difference is the focus: the simple form talks about the result, and the continuous form talks about the activity.

Present perfect simple: the result

have / has + past participle (V3)

  • Finished work with a result now: We've completed the testing phase.
  • How many or how much: We've recorded four of the ten videos.
  • With already, just, yet and so far: HR has just sent the new contracts.

Present perfect continuous: the activity

have / has + been + -ing

  • Work that is still going on: We've been working on the training videos.
  • How long, with for, since and all week: The team has been interviewing candidates since Monday.
  • A recent activity that explains the situation now: The supplier has been having technical problems.

Compare

  • I've written the report. → It's finished. You can read it now.
    I've been writing the report. → That's what I've been doing. It may not be finished.
  • Ana has interviewed six candidates. → How many?
    Ana has been interviewing candidates all morning. → How long?

💡 Quick test

How many? → simple. How long? → continuous.

Some verbs describe states, not actions, so they are not usually used in the continuous form: know, understand, need, belong, and have when it means "own". "I've known about the delay since Monday", not "I've been knowing".

⚠️ Common Errors

  • ✗ I work on this project since Monday. → ✓ I've been working on this project since Monday.
  • ✗ We are waiting for the supplier for two weeks. → ✓ We've been waiting for the supplier for two weeks.
  • ✗ She works here for five years. → ✓ She's worked / She's been working here for five years.

Remember: In Portuguese, the present tense is used here ("Trabalho neste projeto desde segunda"). In English, when something started in the past and continues now, use the present perfect.

Deadlines: by or until?

Portuguese uses "até" for both, but English has two different words.

  • by = no later than. Something happens once, at or before that time. It's the word for deadlines.
    Please send me the figures by Friday. / We expect to finish by the end of the month.
  • until = up to that time. A situation continues, then stops.
    The office is closed until Monday. / The launch has been postponed until March.

Test: if you can say "no later than", use by.
